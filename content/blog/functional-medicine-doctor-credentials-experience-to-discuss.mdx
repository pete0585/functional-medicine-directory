## Start With the Right Question

Most people searching for a Functional Medicine doctor begin with a version of the same question: *who is qualified to help me?* That question is reasonable, but it is also broad enough to be unhelpful on its own. A more workable version is narrower: **what credentials and experience do I want to understand before I decide?**

This article is a decision guide, not a diagnosis, a treatment plan, or a promise about outcomes. It does not name providers, quote prices, or describe licensing rules, because those details vary by location and by individual. Instead, it offers a framework for the conversations you can have directly with a provider or their office, so that you can make a decision that fits your circumstances. Where a question touches on your own health or on legal or regulatory matters, the appropriate step is to consult a qualified professional.

## Why Credentials and Experience Are Two Separate Conversations

It is tempting to treat "credentials" and "experience" as one combined signal of quality. They are related, but they answer different questions.

- **Credentials** answer: *what training, licensure, and formal background does this person hold?*
- **Experience** answers: *what has this person actually done, with whom, and in what settings?*

A provider can be strongly credentialed in one area and relatively new to another. A provider can have years of experience in a setting that differs meaningfully from yours. Neither fact is automatically disqualifying. The point is to understand both dimensions rather than relying on a single impression.

## A Checklist for the Credential Conversation

Use this as a set of questions, not as a scoring rubric. The goal is clarity, not a pass/fail verdict.

1. **Primary professional background.** What is the provider's original clinical or professional training? Functional Medicine is often described as an approach layered onto an existing discipline rather than a standalone replacement for it.
2. **Licensure and scope.** Is the provider licensed in your jurisdiction, and what does that license permit them to do? If you are unsure what a license covers, ask the office to explain, and verify with the relevant authority where appropriate.
3. **Functional Medicine-specific training.** What formal coursework, certification, or continuing education in Functional Medicine has the provider completed? Ask how they describe it in their own words.
4. **Certification status and maintenance.** If a certification is claimed, is it current? Certifications often require renewal, and status can change.
5. **Collaboration and referral relationships.** Does the provider work alongside other clinicians, and how do they handle situations outside their scope?
6. **How credentials are documented.** Can the office point you to where credentials are listed publicly, so you can review them at your own pace?

None of these questions require the provider to disclose anything private. They are ordinary due-diligence questions, and a well-organized practice should be able to answer them or direct you to where the answers live.

## A Checklist for the Experience Conversation

Experience questions are often more revealing than credential questions, because they describe how a provider actually works.

1. **Typical patients and concerns.** What kinds of concerns does this provider commonly work with? You are not asking for a guarantee that they can help you; you are asking whether your situation is familiar territory.
2. **Approach to the first visit.** How is an initial consultation structured? What information do they want from you beforehand?
3. **How plans are developed.** What does the process look like between the first visit and any recommendations? How much of it is conversation versus testing versus other steps?
4. **Follow-up rhythm.** How often do follow-ups typically occur, and what is the purpose of each one?
5. **Communication style.** How do they prefer to communicate between visits, and what should you expect in terms of response times?
6. **Handling of uncertainty.** How does the provider talk about situations where the picture is unclear or where progress is not straightforward?

Question six is worth extra attention. A provider who can speak calmly about uncertainty is often easier to work with than one who promises certainty that no one can honestly offer.

## Hypothetical Examples (Clearly Labeled, Not Real Cases)

The following are **illustrative examples only**. They are not real patients, real providers, or real outcomes. They exist to show how the checklist questions might play out in conversation.

**Example A — The career-changer with strong training, newer practice.**
Imagine a provider who completed a rigorous clinical program and then pursued additional Functional Medicine coursework, but who has only recently begun seeing patients under that model. In a conversation, you might ask how they structure early visits and how they handle situations that fall outside their experience. A thoughtful answer might describe a supervision or consultation arrangement with more experienced colleagues. That is useful information, not a red flag.

**Example B — The veteran clinician new to the Functional Medicine framing.**
Imagine a provider with many years in a conventional setting who has more recently adopted a Functional Medicine approach. You might ask how their practice has changed and what parts of their earlier work they still rely on. The answer can tell you whether the transition is well thought through.

**Example C — The highly specialized provider.**
Imagine a provider whose experience is concentrated in one area. If your concerns fall outside that area, the honest answer may be that they are not the best fit, and they may refer you elsewhere. That is a good outcome for you, even if it is not the outcome you were hoping for.

In each example, the value comes from the conversation itself, not from a label.

## How to Compare Two Providers Without Reducing Them to a Score

When you have notes from two or more conversations, resist the urge to build a single number. Instead, compare along a few dimensions:

- **Clarity of communication.** Which provider explained their process in a way you could follow?
- **Fit with your concerns.** Which one seemed to understand the territory you are in?
- **Practical logistics.** Which one's structure matches your life, including follow-up expectations?
- **Comfort with questions.** Which one welcomed your checklist rather than deflecting it?

## Preparing for the Conversation

A little preparation makes these conversations more productive. Before you call or meet, write down:

- The two or three things you most want to understand about the provider's background.
- Any prior evaluations or records you are comfortable sharing.
- Your own questions about logistics, follow-up, and communication.

## What This Guide Does Not Do

This article does not tell you which provider to choose, does not evaluate any specific provider, and does not describe licensing requirements, which differ by jurisdiction. It does not offer medical advice, and it does not claim that any approach produces any particular result. If you have questions about your own health, or about what a provider is legally permitted to do where you live, those questions belong with qualified professionals.

## A Short Recap

- Treat credentials and experience as two separate conversations.
- Ask concrete questions and take notes.
- Use hypothetical examples as thinking tools, not as evidence.
- Compare providers on communication, fit, logistics, and comfort with questions.
- Consult qualified professionals for anything specific to your health or your jurisdiction.

The goal is not to find a perfect provider. It is to make a decision you understand, with the information you were able to gather directly.
