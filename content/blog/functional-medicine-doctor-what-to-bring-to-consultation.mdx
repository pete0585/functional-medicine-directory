# Functional Medicine Doctor: What to Bring to a Consultation

Walking into a first consultation with a Functional Medicine Doctor can feel different from a standard appointment. These visits often run longer, ask broader questions about your history and daily life, and may involve reviewing many pieces of information at once. That breadth is part of the appeal — but it also means the quality of the conversation depends heavily on what you bring with you.

This guide is a practical decision aid. It focuses on preparation: the documents, context, and questions that help you and a provider make better use of limited time together. It is not medical advice, and it does not promise any particular outcome. For anything related to your individual health, diagnosis, or treatment, consult qualified professionals.

Throughout, examples are clearly labeled as hypothetical illustrations. They are not real cases, and they are not endorsements of any approach.

## Why Preparation Matters

A consultation is a two-way exchange. The provider brings clinical training and a framework for gathering information. You bring the only firsthand account of your history, your daily patterns, and your priorities. When one side of that exchange is thin, the other side has to guess.

Preparation does not mean arriving with a diagnosis in mind or a stack of demands. It means arriving with enough organized context that the provider can ask sharper questions and you can ask better ones in return. Think of it as reducing friction so the conversation can go deeper.

## The Core Checklist: What to Bring

Below is a checklist you can adapt. Not every item will apply to everyone, and you do not need to complete all of it to have a useful visit. Treat it as a menu, not a test.

### 1. Identity and Administrative Basics

- Photo identification and insurance information, if applicable.
- Any intake forms the practice sent you, completed in advance if possible.
- A list of current medications, supplements, and doses — including over-the-counter items and anything taken occasionally.
- Known allergies or sensitivities.
- Emergency contact information.

### 2. Prior Medical Records

- Recent lab or test results, with dates.
- Summaries or notes from other providers, if you can obtain them.
- Imaging reports, if relevant to your concerns.
- A simple timeline of major health events, surgeries, or hospitalizations.

You do not need to bring everything ever recorded. Prioritize what is recent and what relates to the concerns you want to discuss.

### 3. A Written List of Your Concerns

Write down, in plain language, what you most want help with. Rank them if there are several. This single page is often the most valuable thing you bring, because it keeps the conversation anchored when time is short.

### 4. Symptom Patterns and Context

Functional Medicine consultations often explore patterns over time. Consider noting:

- When symptoms tend to appear or worsen.
- How they affect sleep, work, mood, or daily routines.
- Anything that seems to help or worsen them.
- How long you have noticed them.

You are not expected to interpret these patterns. You are simply providing raw material the provider can work with.

### 5. Lifestyle and Daily-Life Context

- A rough picture of your typical sleep schedule.
- General activity patterns.
- Eating patterns, without needing a perfect log.
- Stressors you consider relevant.
- Any major life changes in recent months.

### 6. Goals and Priorities

- What does a good outcome look like to you?
- What matters most right now — relief, understanding, a plan, a second opinion?
- What constraints should the provider know about, such as time, travel, or scheduling?

### 7. Questions You Want Answered

Bring your questions in writing. Examples might include how the provider approaches a first visit, how they communicate findings, and what follow-up typically looks like. You can find more prompts in our guide on [what to ask about ongoing support](https://functionalmddirectory.com/blog/functional-medicine-doctor-what-to-ask-about-ongoing-support).

## How to Organize What You Bring

A little structure goes a long way. Consider three simple folders or sections:

1. **Administrative** — ID, insurance, forms, medication list.
2. **History** — records, test results, timeline.
3. **Your agenda** — concerns, goals, questions.

If you prefer digital, a single note with clear headings works just as well. The goal is to find things quickly when asked, not to produce a polished dossier.

## Hypothetical Examples (Illustrative Only)

These examples are invented to show how preparation can shape a conversation. They are not real patients, and they do not describe outcomes.

**Example A — The scattered first visit.** A hypothetical person arrives with no records and a vague sense of feeling unwell. Much of the appointment is spent reconstructing basic history, leaving little time for the concerns that mattered most. A short written list of concerns and a medication list could have changed the shape of that hour.

**Example B — The over-prepared visit.** Another hypothetical person brings years of records and a long list of questions. The provider spends time sorting through material rather than discussing priorities. Bringing only recent, relevant records plus a ranked list of concerns might have kept the conversation focused.

**Example C — The clear agenda.** A third hypothetical person brings a one-page summary: three concerns in priority order, a current medication list, recent test results, and four questions. The provider can move quickly into substance, and both leave with a shared understanding of next steps.

The lesson in each example is the same: preparation is about focus, not volume.

## Questions to Ask During the Consultation

Beyond what you bring, what you ask shapes the value of the visit. Consider:

- How do you typically structure a first consultation?
- How will you share your findings or impressions with me?
- What information would be most useful for me to track between visits?
- How do you prefer to communicate between appointments?
- What would you like me to do before the next visit, if anything?

For a broader set of prompts, see our guide on [credentials and experience to discuss](https://functionalmddirectory.com/blog/functional-medicine-doctor-credentials-experience-to-discuss).

## What Not to Bring

- Assumptions about what the visit will conclude.
- Pressure to reach a diagnosis in one session.
- A refusal to discuss anything outside a narrow concern — context often matters.
- Unverified claims presented as settled fact.

A consultation is a starting point, not a verdict.

## After the Consultation

Before you leave, it can help to confirm:

- What the provider understood as your main concerns.
- Any recommended next steps, in plain language.
- What you are expected to do, if anything, before follow-up.
- How and when you will hear about any findings.

## A Final Word

Bringing the right materials to a Functional Medicine Doctor consultation is not about performing well or passing a test. It is about giving both you and the provider the best chance to have a focused, honest, and useful conversation. A short written list, recent records, and a handful of thoughtful questions are often enough.

For decisions about your own health, always consult qualified professionals. This article is intended as general preparation guidance, not individualized medical or legal advice.
