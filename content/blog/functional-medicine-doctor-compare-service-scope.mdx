# Functional Medicine Doctor: How to Compare Service Scope

Choosing a Functional Medicine doctor is less about finding a single "best" provider and more about finding a provider whose **scope of services** matches what you are looking for. Two practitioners may both describe themselves as Functional Medicine doctors, yet the practical shape of their work can differ in meaningful ways: how long visits last, whether they offer certain types of testing, how they handle follow-up, what they include in a written plan, and how they coordinate with other clinicians.

This guide walks through a practical way to compare service scope. It is meant to support your own research and conversations. It is not medical advice, and it does not evaluate any specific provider. Where decisions involve your health, finances, or legal rights, consult qualified professionals.

## Why "Service Scope" Matters More Than a Label

The term "Functional Medicine" describes a broad orientation toward care rather than a single standardized service package. Because of that, the label alone tells you relatively little about what a given visit will actually involve.

Two providers might both emphasize root-cause thinking, lifestyle context, and longer conversations. But one might focus almost entirely on initial consultations and written plans, while another offers structured ongoing support, group programs, or coordination with a wider care team. Neither is automatically better. The question is whether the scope fits your situation and preferences.

Comparing scope helps you avoid two common mismatches:

- **Paying for services you will not use.** If a provider bundles extensive follow-up or testing that you do not need or want, you may be paying for scope that does not serve you.
- **Expecting services that are not offered.** If you assume ongoing support is included and it is not, you may feel let down even when the provider delivered exactly what they described.

A clear scope comparison reduces guesswork and helps you ask better questions.

## The Seven Dimensions of Service Scope

When you look at provider information, try to compare along consistent dimensions. The following seven tend to surface the differences that matter most.

### 1. Initial Consultation Structure

- How long is the first visit?
- Is it a single extended session, or split across two appointments?
- Is any part of it completed through a form, portal, or intake questionnaire before the visit?
- Who conducts the visit — the practitioner, a team member, or a combination?

### 2. Assessment Approach

- What kinds of information does the provider gather (history, lifestyle, prior records)?
- Do they describe a structured framework for organizing that information?
- How do they explain their reasoning to you?

This is about process, not about any specific test or treatment. You are comparing how the provider approaches assessment, not whether a particular approach is right for you.

### 3. Written Plans and Documentation

- Do you receive a written summary or plan after visits?
- What does that document typically include (goals, next steps, questions to consider)?
- Is it shared with you in a portal, by email, or on paper?

### 4. Follow-Up and Ongoing Support

- Are follow-up visits included, optional, or separate?
- How are they scheduled — at fixed intervals or as needed?
- Is there a defined way to ask non-urgent questions between visits?
- What happens if your needs change partway through?

### 5. Communication Channels

- Which channels are available (phone, portal messaging, video, in-person)?
- Are there stated expectations about response times?
- Is there a process for urgent concerns that fall outside routine communication?

### 6. Coordination With Other Professionals

- Does the provider describe working alongside your other clinicians?
- Do they share records or summaries when you ask?
- How do they handle referrals if your situation calls for a different kind of expertise?

### 7. Administrative and Access Details

- Where are visits offered (in person, virtual, or both)?
- What are the stated scheduling and cancellation expectations?
- What information is available about how services are billed or structured?

You do not need perfect answers to every question. The goal is to notice where the scope is clear and where it is vague, then follow up.

## A Practical Comparison Checklist

Use this checklist as you review provider information or prepare questions. You can copy it into your own notes and fill it in for each provider you are considering.

**Consultation structure**
- [ ] Length and format of the initial visit are described
- [ ] I understand whether it is one session or several
- [ ] I know who I will be meeting with

**Assessment approach**
- [ ] The provider explains how they gather and organize information
- [ ] I understand what they will ask me to bring or prepare
- [ ] I can describe their overall approach in my own words

**Written plans**
- [ ] I know whether I will receive a written summary
- [ ] I understand what that summary typically includes
- [ ] I know how it will be shared with me

**Follow-up and support**
- [ ] Follow-up options are clearly described
- [ ] I understand how follow-up is scheduled
- [ ] I know how to ask questions between visits

**Communication**
- [ ] Available channels are listed
- [ ] Response-time expectations are stated
- [ ] I know what to do for urgent concerns

**Coordination**
- [ ] The provider describes how they work with other clinicians
- [ ] I understand how records or summaries can be shared
- [ ] I know how referrals are handled

**Access and logistics**
- [ ] Visit locations or virtual options are clear
- [ ] Scheduling and cancellation expectations are stated
- [ ] Billing or service structure information is available

If several boxes are unchecked for a given provider, that is not necessarily a red flag — but it is a signal to ask more questions before deciding.

## Hypothetical Examples (Illustrative Only)

The following examples are **hypothetical and clearly labeled as examples**. They are not descriptions of real providers, real outcomes, or real prices. They are here to show how scope comparisons might look in practice.

**Example A — The consultation-focused provider.**

Imagine a provider whose stated scope centers on a single extended initial consultation, a written summary afterward, and optional follow-up that you schedule if you choose. Their materials emphasize the depth of the first conversation and the clarity of the written plan. If you are looking for a thorough starting point and prefer to decide about follow-up later, this scope may align with your preferences. If you already know you want regular check-ins, you might ask how those would be arranged and whether they are part of the standard scope or a separate arrangement.

**Example B — The structured-program provider.**

Now imagine a provider whose scope is organized as a defined program: an initial visit, a set number of follow-up sessions, and a specific communication channel between visits. Their materials describe the program as a whole rather than as individual pieces. If you value a clear structure and predictable touchpoints, this may appeal to you. If you prefer flexibility, you might ask whether the program can be adjusted and how changes are handled.

**Example C — The collaborative-care provider.**

Consider a provider whose scope explicitly includes coordination with your other clinicians and a stated process for sharing summaries. Their materials describe how they fit alongside a broader care team. If you already work with several professionals and want someone who will communicate across that group, this scope may be relevant. If you prefer a single point of contact, you might ask how much coordination is typical and whether it is optional.

In each example, the point is not that one scope is superior. The point is that the scope differs, and knowing the difference helps you choose.

## Questions to Ask When Scope Is Unclear

If a provider's information leaves gaps, a short, direct question usually helps. Consider asking:

1. "What is included in the initial consultation, and what happens afterward?"
2. "Do you provide a written summary, and what does it typically cover?"
3. "How is follow-up arranged, and is it part of the standard scope?"
4. "How do you prefer to communicate between visits?"
5. "Do you coordinate with other clinicians, and how does that work?"
6. "What should I do if my needs change after we begin?"

You are not expected to know every question in advance. Asking a few focused ones is often enough to clarify scope.

## Common Pitfalls in Scope Comparison

- **Assuming all Functional Medicine doctors offer the same services.** Scope varies widely; compare specifics rather than labels.
- **Confusing a longer visit with a broader scope.** A long initial visit is one dimension, not the whole picture.
- **Overlooking follow-up.** Ongoing support is often the most important scope difference, and it is easy to miss.
- **Skipping the logistics.** Scheduling, communication, and coordination details affect your day-to-day experience.
- **Relying on a single source.** Combine what you read with what you ask directly.
