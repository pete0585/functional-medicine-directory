## Why cancellation policies deserve a place on your question list

When people research a Functional Medicine Doctor, they usually focus on approach, credentials, and whether the provider seems like a good fit. Cancellation terms tend to come up later, often after a first appointment is booked. That order of operations can create avoidable friction.

Cancellation policies are not a sign that a practice is unfriendly or that the relationship is transactional. They are logistics. Scheduling, rescheduling, and no-show rules exist because appointment time is a limited resource. Understanding those rules before you commit helps you decide whether a provider's structure matches your life.

This guide is about questions, not verdicts. It does not evaluate any specific provider, and it does not offer clinical or legal advice. Policies vary widely, and the only authoritative source for a given practice is that practice. Where a question touches on your own health, finances, or legal rights, consult a qualified professional.

## Start with the basics: what counts as a cancellation

A surprising number of disagreements begin with definitions. Ask directly:

- What counts as a cancellation versus a reschedule?
- Is there a difference between canceling a first visit and canceling a follow-up?
- Does the policy differ for in-person visits and virtual visits?
- How is a "late" cancellation defined — by hours, by business days, or by something else?
- What happens if I need to change the time rather than cancel entirely?

Getting these definitions in writing, or at least in a message you can refer back to, reduces the chance of a misunderstanding later.

## The cancellation policy checklist

Use this as a starting point. You do not need to ask every question in one call, but the answers should be findable before you commit.

**Timing and notice**
- How much notice is required to cancel or reschedule without a fee?
- Is the notice period measured in calendar days, business days, or hours?
- Does the clock start when you send the request or when the practice confirms it?

**Fees and billing**
- Is there a cancellation fee, and how is it calculated?
- Does the fee depend on the type or length of the appointment?
- If a fee applies, when is it charged, and how?
- Are there circumstances under which a fee is waived?

**Communication channels**
- Which methods count as an official cancellation (phone, portal message, email, text)?
- Who confirms that the cancellation was received?
- What should I do if I do not get a confirmation?

**Emergencies and exceptions**
- How does the practice handle sudden illness, family emergencies, or weather?
- Is there a process for documenting an emergency after the fact?
- Are exceptions handled case by case, or is there a stated rule?

**Rescheduling**
- If I reschedule within the notice window, is there any charge?
- How far out are new appointments typically available?
- Can I reschedule more than once?

**Missed appointments**
- How is a no-show defined?
- Is a no-show treated differently from a late cancellation?
- What happens after a first no-show versus a repeat pattern?

**Payment methods and prepayment**
- If I prepaid, how is a refund or credit handled after a cancellation?
- Are credits transferable or time-limited?

**Written documentation**
- Where can I find the policy in writing?
- Is it part of an intake packet or a separate document?

## Clearly labeled hypothetical examples

The following are illustrative examples only. They are not descriptions of real practices, and they are not recommendations. They are meant to show how the same question can produce very different answers.

**Example A — A practice with a 48-hour rule.**
A hypothetical practice asks for 48 hours' notice to cancel or reschedule without a fee. A person who wakes up sick the morning of an appointment would fall inside that window. The useful question is not whether the rule is fair, but whether the practice has a stated process for acute illness. If the answer is "it depends," that is worth knowing before you book.

**Example B — A practice with a same-day courtesy window.**
A hypothetical practice allows same-day changes without a fee if you contact them before a certain time. For someone with unpredictable work or caregiving responsibilities, that flexibility may matter more than other features of the practice.

**Example C — A prepaid package.**
A hypothetical practice sells a package of visits at a discount. A person who needs to cancel one visit might want to know whether the unused visit becomes a credit, expires, or is forfeited. The answer shapes the real cost of the package, not just its sticker price.

**Example D — A virtual-only practice.**
A hypothetical virtual practice may have different rules for technology failures versus voluntary cancellations. Asking how those are distinguished can prevent a frustrating dispute.

**Example E — A practice with a waitlist.**
A hypothetical practice fills cancellations from a waitlist. A person who cancels late might still be charged, even though the slot was refilled. Whether that matters to you depends on how the policy is explained up front.

## How to compare policies across providers

Once you have answers from two or three providers, patterns become visible. A few comparison angles can help:

- **Predictability.** Is the policy stated clearly and consistently, or does it seem to shift depending on the situation?
- **Fit with your schedule.** A strict policy may be perfectly workable if your schedule is stable. It may be a poor fit if your weeks change unpredictably.
- **Fit with your communication style.** If you prefer written confirmation, a practice that relies on phone calls may create friction.
- **Transparency.** A policy you can read before booking is easier to plan around than one you learn about after the fact.
- **Consistency with other policies.** Cancellation terms often sit alongside scheduling, payment, and communication policies. Reading them together gives a fuller picture.

None of these angles tells you which provider is "better." They help you match a structure to your circumstances.

## Questions to ask about accessibility and special circumstances

If you have accessibility needs, caregiving responsibilities, or a health condition that affects your ability to attend appointments, cancellation policy is not a minor detail. Consider asking:

- Are there accommodations for recurring or unpredictable needs?
- Is there a process for notifying the practice when something changes at short notice?
- Can a caregiver or advocate communicate on your behalf?
- Are there alternative formats for the policy, such as a large-print or screen-reader-friendly version?

These questions are about access, not about seeking exceptions. A practice that has thought about them is often easier to work with over time.

## What to do with the answers

Once you have the information, a few practical steps can help:

1. **Write it down.** Keep the policy details in one place, alongside your appointment notes.
2. **Confirm in writing.** If a policy was explained verbally, ask for a written summary.
3. **Note the notice window.** Put the relevant deadline in your calendar when you book.
4. **Ask about exceptions before you need one.** It is easier to ask a general question early than to negotiate after a missed appointment.
5. **Revisit if circumstances change.** A policy that worked a year ago may not fit your life now.

## Where this fits in provider selection

Cancellation policy is one part of a larger decision. It sits alongside questions about availability, communication, referral arrangements, and how a practice describes its approach. None of these alone determines a good fit, but together they shape what working with a provider is actually like.

If you are comparing providers, it can help to look at these topics in sequence: first, what you need; second, what each practice offers; third, how the logistics line up. Cancellation terms belong in that third category, and they are easier to evaluate when you have already clarified the first two.

## A short recap

- Define what counts as a cancellation before you book.
- Ask about notice periods, fees, and how they are applied.
- Confirm which communication channels are official.
- Ask how emergencies and exceptions are handled.
- Get the policy in writing and keep it with your notes.
- Revisit the policy if your circumstances change.
