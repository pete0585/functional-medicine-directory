## Why a Question List Matters

Meeting a Functional Medicine Doctor for the first time can feel like a lot to manage. There is often a long intake process, a detailed history, and a conversation that may cover sleep, digestion, stress, energy, and more. Without a plan, it is easy to leave the appointment realizing you forgot to ask about the things that mattered most to you.

A written question list is a simple tool that helps you stay organized. It keeps the conversation focused on your goals, reduces the chance you will forget something important, and gives you a record you can refer back to. This article is a practical decision guide. It does not offer medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment recommendations. For anything related to your personal health, please consult a qualified professional.

## What Makes a Question List Useful

A useful question list is not a long, unfiltered brain dump. It is a short, prioritized set of questions grouped by theme. The goal is to help you have a clearer conversation, not to overwhelm yourself or the person you are meeting.

A few principles help:

- **Prioritize.** Put the two or three questions that matter most at the top. If time runs short, you will have covered the essentials.
- **Group by theme.** Cluster related questions together, such as questions about the first visit, questions about how a plan is built, and questions about logistics.
- **Write open-ended questions.** Questions that start with "how," "what," or "can you walk me through" tend to invite more useful answers than yes/no questions.
- **Keep it to one page.** A single page is easier to scan during a conversation than a multi-page document.
- **Leave space for notes.** A few blank lines under each question let you jot down what you hear.

## A Checklist to Build Your List

Use this checklist as a starting point. Adjust it to fit your own situation.

- [ ] Write down your top two or three goals for the visit in plain language.
- [ ] List any questions you have about how the first visit is structured.
- [ ] Note questions about how a plan or set of next steps is typically developed.
- [ ] Add questions about communication, follow-up, and how you will stay in touch.
- [ ] Include logistics questions, such as scheduling, visit length, and what to bring.
- [ ] Mark which questions are must-ask and which are nice-to-ask.
- [ ] Read your list out loud once to check that it sounds clear.
- [ ] Bring a pen and a copy of the list to your appointment.

## Question Themes to Consider

Below are themes you may want to include. These are general categories, not a script. Choose the ones that fit you.

### Questions About the First Visit

- What does a typical first visit look like, and roughly how long does it take?
- What information would be most helpful for me to bring?
- How should I describe my goals so we can make the most of the time?

### Questions About How a Plan Is Built

- Can you walk me through how you typically approach building a plan with someone?
- How do you decide what to focus on first?
- What role do my own preferences play in shaping next steps?

### Questions About Communication and Follow-Up

- How do people usually stay in touch between visits?
- What is the best way to reach you if I have a question?
- How often do follow-up conversations typically happen?

### Questions About Logistics

- What should I bring to the first appointment?
- How far in advance do appointments usually need to be scheduled?
- Are there any forms I should complete ahead of time?

## How to Use Your List During the Visit

Having a list is only useful if you actually use it. A few habits help:

1. **Start with your top questions.** Open the conversation by saying you have a short list and would like to make sure you cover the most important items.
2. **Take brief notes.** You do not need to write everything down. A few key phrases next to each question are enough to jog your memory later.
3. **Ask for clarification.** If an answer is unclear, it is fine to say so and ask for a simpler explanation.
4. **Leave room for questions you did not anticipate.** A good conversation often surfaces new questions. Add them to your list as they come up.
5. **Review your list afterward.** Once the visit is over, look back at what you wrote and note anything you still want to follow up on.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Trying to cover too much.** A list with thirty questions is hard to use. Aim for a focused set.
- **Writing only closed questions.** Yes/no questions can shut down a conversation. Mix in open-ended ones.
- **Forgetting to prioritize.** If you do not mark what matters most, you may run out of time before you get to it.
- **Not leaving space for notes.** A list without room to write is only half as useful.
- **Treating the list as a script.** It is a guide, not a rigid plan. Let the conversation flow.
