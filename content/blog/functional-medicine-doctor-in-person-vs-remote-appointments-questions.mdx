# Functional Medicine Doctor: In-Person vs. Remote Appointments — Questions to Ask

Choosing a Functional Medicine Doctor involves more than finding someone with the right expertise. You also need to decide how you want to work with them. In-person visits and remote appointments each offer a different set of trade-offs, and the right fit depends on your circumstances, your comfort level, and the practical realities of your daily life.

This guide is designed to help you think through that decision. It does not recommend one format over the other, and it does not offer medical advice. Instead, it gives you a structured way to ask better questions — so you can make a choice that aligns with your needs and preferences.

## Why the Format Matters

Functional Medicine is often described as a patient-centered approach that looks at the whole person rather than isolated symptoms. That philosophy can shape how appointments are structured, how much time you spend with a provider, and how follow-up is handled. The format of your appointments — in person or remote — can affect each of those elements.

For some people, being in the same room as a provider feels more personal and allows for a hands-on element that remote care cannot replicate. For others, remote appointments reduce travel time, make scheduling easier, and open up access to providers who may not be located nearby.

Neither option is inherently better. The goal is to understand what each format can and cannot offer, and to ask questions that surface those differences before you commit.

## Questions to Ask About In-Person Appointments

If you are leaning toward in-person visits, consider asking a provider or their office the following:

- **What does a typical in-person visit include?** Ask how the time is structured, whether there is a physical examination component, and how much of the visit is dedicated to conversation versus other activities.
- **How is the office set up?** If your needs involve accessibility, sensory considerations, or privacy, ask about the environment and any accommodations available.
- **What is the travel and wait-time expectation?** In-person visits may involve commuting, parking, and time in a waiting room. Ask how the office manages scheduling so you can plan accordingly.
- **Are there services that are only available in person?** Some providers may offer certain types of appointments or assessments only at their physical location. Ask which services fall into that category.
- **How are follow-ups handled?** Ask whether follow-up visits are expected to be in person, remote, or a mix of both.
- **What happens if you need to reschedule?** Ask about the office's policy for cancellations and rescheduling, and whether there are any associated considerations.

These questions help you understand the practical shape of in-person care before you decide whether it fits your routine.

## Questions to Ask About Remote Appointments

If remote appointments are appealing — or necessary — consider asking these questions:

- **What platform is used, and what do I need to participate?** Ask whether the appointment happens by video, phone, or another method, and what technical setup is required on your end.
- **How is privacy handled?** Ask how the provider protects your information during a remote visit and what steps you should take on your side.
- **What can and cannot be done remotely?** Ask whether any part of the visit requires an in-person component, and how the provider handles situations where physical presence would normally be expected.
- **How do you handle technical problems?** Ask what happens if the connection drops or the platform fails during your appointment.
- **How are follow-ups and between-visit questions managed?** Ask whether you can reach the provider or their team between appointments, and through what channels.
- **What are the scheduling options?** Remote appointments may offer more flexibility, but ask about time zones, availability, and how appointments are confirmed.
- **Is there a backup plan if remote care is not working for me?** Ask how the provider would handle a transition to in-person care if needed.

These questions help you assess whether remote care will feel workable and supportive in practice.

## A Checklist for Comparing the Two Formats

Use this checklist as a starting point. You can adapt it to your own priorities.

**Access and logistics**
- [ ] How far is the office, and how long would travel take?
- [ ] Do I have reliable internet and a private space for remote visits?
- [ ] Which format fits better with my work, caregiving, or other responsibilities?

**Comfort and communication**
- [ ] Do I feel more at ease speaking with a provider in person or through a screen?
- [ ] How does the provider communicate between visits?
- [ ] Am I comfortable with the technology involved in remote care?

**Scope of services**
- [ ] Are there any services I need that are only offered in person?
- [ ] Are there any services I need that are only offered remotely?
- [ ] How does the provider handle situations that require physical presence?

**Follow-up and continuity**
- [ ] How often are follow-up appointments expected?
- [ ] Can follow-ups be remote, in person, or both?
- [ ] What is the process for rescheduling or canceling?

**Practical policies**
- [ ] What is the cancellation policy?
- [ ] How are appointments confirmed and reminders sent?
- [ ] What should I do if I have a question between visits?

This checklist is not exhaustive, but it can help you organize your thinking and prepare questions for a provider.

## Hypothetical Examples (Clearly Labeled as Examples)

The following scenarios are hypothetical and are meant only to illustrate how someone might think through the decision. They are not based on real people, real providers, or real outcomes.

**Example 1: A person with a demanding job and limited time**

Suppose someone works long hours and has difficulty taking time off for appointments. They might prioritize remote appointments because they reduce travel time and can be scheduled more flexibly. However, they might also ask whether any part of their care requires in-person visits, and how the provider handles follow-ups remotely. If the provider cannot accommodate their schedule remotely, they might reconsider.

**Example 2: A person who prefers a hands-on approach**

Suppose someone feels more comfortable with in-person visits and wants to establish a strong rapport with a provider in the same room. They might ask about the office environment, the length of visits, and whether follow-ups can also be in person. If the provider primarily offers remote care, they might look for another provider whose format matches their preference.

**Example 3: A person who lives far from any provider**

Suppose someone lives in an area with limited access to Functional Medicine Doctors. They might rely on remote appointments to connect with a provider outside their immediate area. They would want to ask about licensing, since a provider may only be able to work with patients in certain locations. They would also ask how the provider handles situations that might normally require an in-person examination.

**Example 4: A person who is unsure and wants to try both**

Suppose someone is not sure which format they prefer. They might ask a provider whether they can start with a remote consultation and then decide whether to switch to in-person visits. They would want to know how the provider handles transitions between formats and whether there are any differences in how care is delivered.

In each example, the key is to ask questions early so you can make an informed choice.

## What to Do With Your Answers

Once you have gathered information, compare it against your own priorities. There is no single right answer. Some people will find that in-person visits are essential for their comfort and communication style. Others will find that remote appointments make care more accessible and sustainable.

You may also find that a mix of both works best — for example, an initial in-person visit followed by remote follow-ups. Ask providers whether they offer that kind of flexibility.

If you are unsure, consider starting with a consultation and asking directly about the format. A provider who is transparent about their approach and willing to answer your questions is often a good sign, regardless of the format they offer.
