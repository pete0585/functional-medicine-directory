## Why privacy belongs on your provider-selection checklist

When people research a Functional Medicine doctor, they often focus on approach, appointment format, and how a first consultation is structured. Privacy and information sharing deserve a place on that same list. Functional Medicine visits can involve detailed conversations about sleep, stress, digestion, mood, family history, and lifestyle. That kind of context is exactly what makes the conversation useful, and it is also exactly the kind of information you may want handled with care.

This guide is not legal advice, and it is not individualized medical advice. Privacy rules differ by location, by the type of professional you see, and by how a practice is organized. Use the questions below as a starting point, and consult qualified professionals — including the practice itself, your own legal or insurance advisors, or a relevant licensing or privacy authority — for answers that apply to your situation.

## The core distinction: clinical privacy vs. business information sharing

It helps to separate two things that often get blurred together in conversation.

- **Clinical privacy** covers how your health information is collected, stored, used in your care, and disclosed. This is usually governed by health-privacy laws that apply to certain providers and organizations, and by the practice's own policies.
- **Business information sharing** covers things like marketing lists, newsletters, review requests, third-party scheduling tools, analytics on a website, or a practice's relationship with labs and technology vendors.

A practice can be careful with one and loose with the other. Ask about both.

## A checklist of questions to ask before you choose

You do not need to ask every question in one call. Many people ask a few at the front desk or by email, then go deeper during a consultation. Consider keeping notes so you can compare providers consistently.

**About collection and use**

1. What information do you collect before, during, and after a visit, and what do you use each part for?
2. Do you record visits in writing, by audio, or by video? If so, who can access those records?
3. Do you use my information for anything beyond my care — for example, newsletters, testimonials, or marketing?
4. If you would like to use my story or comments publicly, how do you ask, and can I decline without affecting my care?

**About sharing with others**

5. Which labs, pharmacies, imaging centers, or technology vendors do you work with, and what information do you send them?
6. Do you share information with my primary care provider or other clinicians? Is that automatic, or do you ask me first?
7. Do you share information with family members, employers, schools, or insurers? Under what circumstances?
8. Are there situations where you are required to disclose information without my permission? Can you explain those in plain language?

**About storage and access**

9. Where is my information stored — paper files, an electronic record, a patient portal, or a combination?
10. Who inside the practice can see my record, and how is that access limited?
11. How long do you keep records, and what happens to them if the practice closes or I stop being a patient?
12. How do I request a copy of my records, ask for a correction, or ask a question about privacy?

**About communication preferences**

13. Can we communicate by email or text, and what are the trade-offs you want me to understand?
14. If I use a patient portal, what is it used for, and who monitors messages?
15. What is your process if I want to limit certain communications — for example, appointment reminders only?

**About remote and third-party tools**

16. For remote visits, what platform do you use, and how is the session protected?
17. Are any scheduling, payment, or messaging tools operated by outside companies? What do those companies receive?
18. If I complete an online form or questionnaire, where does that information go?

## Clearly labeled hypothetical examples

The following are hypothetical illustrations only. They are not real practices, real people, or claims about how any specific provider operates. They are meant to show how the same question can lead to different follow-ups.

**Example A — The marketing question.**

*Hypothetical:* A person asks whether their comments might be used in a testimonial. The practice says it asks for written permission and offers a version that removes identifying details. The person then asks who reviews the final wording and whether they can withdraw permission later. These are reasonable follow-ups, and the answers help the person decide whether they are comfortable.

**Example B — The lab question.**

*Hypothetical:* A person learns that a practice works with an outside lab. The person asks what information is sent to the lab, whether the lab keeps a copy, and how results come back to the practice. The person also asks what happens if they prefer a different lab. The answers may or may not fit the person's preferences, but the questions make the arrangement visible.

**Example C — The family question.**

*Hypothetical:* A person wants a partner involved in appointments. They ask whether the partner can join a visit, what the partner will be able to see in a portal, and how the practice handles requests from family members who call on the person's behalf. Clarifying this in advance can prevent awkward moments later.

**Example D — The remote-visit question.**

*Hypothetical:* A person considering a remote appointment asks which platform is used, whether the session is recorded, and how notes are stored afterward. They also ask what happens if the connection drops mid-visit. These are practical questions that affect both privacy and continuity of care.

## How to read the answers you get

You are not looking for a single "correct" answer. You are looking for clarity, consistency, and a willingness to explain. A few things worth noticing:

- **Plain language.** A practice that can explain its approach without jargon is often easier to work with.
- **Written policies.** Ask whether privacy practices are documented and where you can read them.
- **Consistency.** If two people at the same practice give very different answers, that is worth clarifying.
- **Comfort with questions.** You are allowed to ask. A practice that welcomes the conversation is giving you useful information about the relationship.

If something is unclear, you can ask for it in writing. If you still have concerns, consider consulting a qualified professional who can advise on your specific situation.

## A short pre-visit privacy checklist

Before your first appointment, consider whether you can answer these for yourself:

- I know what information the practice collects and why.
- I know who inside and outside the practice may see my information.
- I know how to request my records and how to ask a privacy question.
- I know how the practice communicates with me and what I can opt out of.
- I know which outside tools or labs are involved and what they receive.
- I know what happens if I want to change providers or limit certain communications.

If any item is unclear, that is a good signal to ask before you proceed.

## A closing note

Privacy conversations are not about suspicion. They are about informed choice.
